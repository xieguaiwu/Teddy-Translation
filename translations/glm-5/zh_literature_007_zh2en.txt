"The Five Marquises"
In the second year of the Heping era of Emperor Cheng of Han (Liu Ao) (27 BC), the five brothers of the imperial kin by marriage—Wang Tan, Wang Fengshi, Wang Gen, Wang Li, and Wang Shang—were enfeoffed as marquises on the same day, and were referred to at the time as the "Five Marquises." According to the *Biographies of Wandering Knights* in the *Book of Han*, the "Five Marquises" kept many Confucian knights-errant, among whom the great knight-errant Lou Hu (Junqing) was the most trusted, serving as the "honored guest of the Five Marquises."

The aroma of millet porridge filled the small room. The woman lifted the lid of the pot. I was surprised to find that there was a lot of porridge in the pot, enough to fill three bowls. The woman brought over three large black bowls from the corner and scooped the porridge into them with a wooden spoon with a scorched rim. Scoop after scoop after scoop, scoop after scoop after scoop, scoop after scoop after scoop, filling the three large bowls, and there was still a lot left in the pot. I was puzzled, pleasantly surprised, and confused. Could all this porridge really have been boiled from just a few dozen grains of millet? What kind of person was this woman, really? Was she a demon? Was she a goddess? The two foxes that had rushed into the temple hall during the torrential rain were drawn by the aroma of the porridge and walked right into our small room. The vixen was in front, the male fox behind, and toddling between them were three furry little foxes. They looked endearingly naive and very cute. The saying that beasts like to give birth during thunderstorms and heavy rain was indeed true. The two large foxes squatted in front of the pot, occasionally looking up at the woman, their eyes flashing with a begging light; occasionally staring into the pot, their eyes flashing with a greedy light. Their stomachs made rumbling sounds, the sound of hunger. The three little foxes nudged under the vixen's belly, looking for teats. The male fox's eyes were moist, his gaze vivid, looking as if he might speak at any moment. I knew that if he opened his mouth to speak, what he would say. The woman looked at the great monk, the great monk sighed, and pushed the large bowl in front of him toward the vixen. The woman followed suit and pushed the bowl of porridge in front of her toward the male fox. After nodding in thanks to the great monk and the woman, the two foxes began to eat with loud smacks. The porridge was very hot, and they ate carefully, their eyes brimming with tears. I felt very awkward, looking at the porridge in front of me, not knowing whether I should eat it or not. The great monk said: Eat. This was definitely the best porridge I had ever eaten, and I would never taste such good porridge again. The two foxes and I each ate three bowls of porridge. The foxes burped, took the little foxes, and walked away unsteadily. And at this time, I noticed that the pot was completely clean, without a single grain of rice left. I felt apologetic, but the great monk had already sat on the bed, twirling his prayer beads, seemingly falling asleep. That woman sat in front of the coal briquette stove, playing with an iron skewer in her hand. The faint fire of the stove reflected on her face, making it so vivid and spirited. She smiled, as if recalling beautiful past events, or as if remembering nothing and thinking of nothing. I stroked my bulging belly and heard the sound of the little foxes suckling coming from the temple hall outside. I couldn't hear the sound of the kittens suckling in the tree hollow, but I seemed to see them suckling too. I also had a strong desire to suckle, but where were my nipples? I didn't feel sleepy at all. To resist the desire to suckle, I said: Great Monk, I will continue speaking.

I grew up in a remote area and knew nothing about Manchu and Han distinctions. I had only seen the words "Manchu-Han Banquet" on restaurant signs, which never aroused any questions in me. It was common to hear people tell stories of "the current dynasty," but I had never heard of literary inquisitions, and the grand occasions of Emperor Qianlong's southern tours [376] were rarely recounted. What was spoken of most was "fighting the Long Mao." There was an elderly female servant in our house who said she was already in her teens during the Long Mao rebellion. She was the one who told me the most stories about the Long Mao, but she made no distinction between the righteous and the wicked. She only said there were three most terrifying things: one was naturally the "Long Mao," another was the "Short Mao," and the third was the "Flower-Green-Heads" [377]. It was only later that I realized the latter two were actually government soldiers, but in the experience of the ignorant folk, they were no different from the Long Mao. It was actually a few scholars who pointed out to me the wickedness of the Long Mao. My family had several county gazetteers, and occasionally flipping through them, I would find one or two whole volumes of rosters of martyrs and heroic women who died for their loyalty. Several people from my own clan had also been killed, and were later posthumously granted the hereditary title of "Cloud Cavalryman" [378]. Thus, I firmly established the wickedness of the Long Mao in my mind. However, as the saying goes, "The mind's thoughts are like surging waves" [379]. Over time, through my own life experience, corroborated by the old servant's accounts, I actually could not determine whether the murderers of those martyrs and heroic women were the Long Mao, or the "Short Mao" and the "Flower-Green-Heads." I truly envy the happiness of the sage who, "at forty, had no perplexities" [380].
