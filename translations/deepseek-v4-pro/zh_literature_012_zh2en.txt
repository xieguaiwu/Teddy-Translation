Although I’m not very old, I know quite a few people who have taken their own lives. From what I can remember, the leadership often bears an even greater hatred toward the dead than toward the living, heaping a slew of accusations upon them — cutting themselves off from those above, cutting themselves off from the people, leaving a stench for ten thousand years, and so on. But these accusations don’t frighten the dead. No matter what, they leave the leadership with a huge dilemma: how could such black-hearted, dog-livered wretches bear to renounce such a beautiful life in this world. Take Hongfu, for instance. If she truly sought death out of grief for losing her husband, that might be forgivable. The fear is that her words didn’t match her heart. If that’s the case, we’d have to ask her clearly before she’s completely dead. But we’ll leave that story for later. What I want to tell now is how Hongfu stirred up misunderstandings in Chang’an. These matters, when I recount them, are captivating to hear, because my greatest specialty is also stirring up misunderstandings.

Jianyun was utterly stirred. Such a look and such words conquered his heart completely. Deeply moved, even with a sense of reverence, he called out, “Second Miss,” and then, choking up, he said, “I will never forget your words. No one has ever spoken to me like this before, no one ever —”

“Grandpa, you said ‘singing songs,’ and just yesterday in my dreams I heard the most beautiful singing, soft and lingering. It was as if I followed that voice everywhere, flying to the sheer cliff halfway across the stream, picking a big bunch of saxifrage. Once I had it, I had no idea who I was supposed to give it to. I slept so well, such a fascinating dream!”

On that very afternoon as well, the presidium of the congress announced the small-group discussions, and the attendants brought over the three newspapers subscribed for the delegates during the session. Those who were speaking went on speaking, and those who weren’t opened their newspapers. Zhuang Zhidie first read the arts page in the third section of the provincial newspaper, then glanced at the city paper — almost all of the front pages were filled with various reports on the congress. Finding it uninteresting, he picked up the third newspaper, called Weekend, and was instantly drawn to a news item. The headline read: Work Starts Leisurely at City Government Compound, Barely Half the Staff Arriving After Half an Hour. The content was actually an investigation conducted by a reporter from this paper at the compound gate on a certain morning: how many people arrived after ten minutes, how many after twenty, and how many after half an hour. How many bureau chiefs were late, how many deputy mayors were late. Instantly the meeting broke into buzzing chatter, and the topic shifted from discussing the mayor’s government work report to arguing about this report. Zhuang Zhidie listened for a while; it was nothing more than a chaotic uproar of grumbling. Feeling utterly bored, he returned to his room and dialed home to ask if anything important had come up. Liu Yue answered, asking repeatedly, “Who is it? Who is it?” Just as Zhuang Zhidie was about to speak, sound of playful chatter came over the line. Curious to hear who was fooling around, he remained silent. Liu Yue over there said, “Psycho!” and clack, put down the receiver. Zhuang Zhidie dialed again. Without asking who it was, Liu Yue yelled, “Wrong number! This is the crematorium!” and hung up again. Fuming, Zhuang Zhidie dialed once more, and the moment someone picked up, he scolded, “Liu Yue, is that how you answer the phone at home?!” Recognizing the voice, Liu Yue said hurriedly, “Teacher Zhuang, is that really you? These last few days while you’ve been away, there have been dozens of calls a day looking for you. I’d say you weren’t here, and a while later another call would come, so Big Sister told me to answer and say it was a wrong number. I never expected I’d miss your call by mistake.” Zhuang Zhidie was still angry. “Who was talking with you there?” Liu Yue said, “It’s Hong Jiang. He just came looking for you. Do you want to speak to him?” Hong Jiang’s voice came on the line, at first mumbling and unclear, then, getting to the matter of the bookstore, he immediately said that the manuscript had been printed for two days already and distributed to retail outlets everywhere, and sales were extremely good. Hong Jiang went on chattering for a long while, while Zhuang Zhidie didn’t say a word. Finally Hong Jiang said, “Teacher Zhuang, are you listening?” Zhuang Zhidie said, “Mm.” Hong Jiang said, “This time we’ve hit the jackpot. I’ve roughly calculated it: we invested a hundred thousand and can net thirty thousand! Judging by the current market, I’m thinking in ten days or half a month we should print another ten thousand copies. So I wanted to ask about entertaining that man Jia in the postal distribution department. This is someone we can’t afford to offend. Besides the proper distribution channels, he holds a map of underground distribution contacts in his hands. If you think this idea works, could you meet him in person? Tomorrow, or the day after?” Zhuang Zhidie said, “I’m busy. Tell your teacher’s wife about it.” With that, he hung up, stretched out on the bed, and slept right until supper time.

Mother was stunned, her heavy head drooping sadly. Suddenly she burst into loud wailing. Crying, she went into the house. Sima Ting sighed, squatting under the pear tree to smoke.

Perhaps every birth of the webbed infant marked the start of another agonizing era in the family’s history — otherwise why would Grandpa have been so terrified? His face was deathly pale, and the sallow, singed whiskers on his chin quivered and curled like tufts of thatch in a flame, curling and quivering. His tall frame swayed back and forth, as if he might collapse at any moment and split into a heap of garbage.
