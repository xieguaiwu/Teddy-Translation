"The Five Marquises"

In the second year of the Heping era under Emperor Cheng of Han (Liu Ao, 27 BCE), five brothers of the Wang clan—Wang Tan, Wang Fengshi, Wang Gen, Wang Li, and Wang Shang—were all enfeoffed as marquises on the same day. At the time, they were known as "The Five Marquises." According to the "Biographies of Gallants" in the *Book of Han*, the Five Marquises kept many Confucian scholars and knights-errant in their service. Among them, the great gallant Lou Hu (Junqing) was the most trusted and was a "respected guest of the Five Marquises."

The fragrance of millet porridge filled the small room. The woman lifted the lid of the pot. To my surprise, I found there was a great deal of porridge in the pot, enough to fill three bowls. The woman brought three large black bowls from a corner of the room and ladled porridge into them with a wooden spoon whose edges were charred. One scoop after another, one scoop after another, one scoop after another—she filled three big bowls, and there was still plenty left in the pot. I was baffled, delighted, and bewildered. Could all this porridge really have been made from those few dozen grains of millet? Who was this woman? A sorceress? An immortal? The two foxes that had rushed into the temple during the pouring rain, drawn by the scent of the porridge, boldly entered our little room. The vixen was in front, the dog fox behind, and between them tottered three furry little cubs. They were adorably clumsy and innocent. In moments of lightning, thunder, and torrential rain, animals tend to give birth—truly, that saying is no lie. The two large foxes crouched in front of the pot. From time to time they raised their heads to look at the woman, their eyes glistening with a pleading light; then they would fix their gaze upon the pot, their eyes gleaming with greedy desire. From their bellies came rumbling sounds—the sounds of hunger. The three little cubs burrowed beneath the vixen’s belly, searching for teats. The dog fox’s eyes were moist and expressive, as if he were about to speak at any moment. I knew what he would say if he could talk. The woman exchanged a glance with the senior monk. With a sigh, the monk pushed the big bowl in front of him toward the vixen. Following suit, the woman pushed her own bowl of porridge toward the dog fox. The two foxes nodded gratefully toward the monk and the woman, then lapped their food noisily. The porridge was hot, and they ate cautiously, tears in their eyes. I sat there awkwardly, eyeing the porridge before me, uncertain if I should eat or not. "You go ahead and eat," said the senior monk. That was surely the best porridge I have ever eaten, and never again will I taste such good porridge. The foxes and I each ate three bowls. Full and burping, they left with their little cubs, swaying unsteadily. At that moment I noticed the pot was utterly clean, without a grain of millet left. I felt deeply apologetic, but the senior monk was already sitting on the bed, fingering his rosary beads as if falling asleep. The woman sat before the briquette stove, idly playing with an iron skewer. The faint glow of the fire flickered across her face; she was so vivid and striking. She smiled, as if recalling pleasant memories or perhaps remembering and thinking of nothing at all. Rubbing my bulging belly, I heard the sounds of the little fox cubs suckling from beyond, in the temple hall. I could not hear the kittens suckling inside the hollow tree, but I could almost see them suckling as well. A powerful urge to nurse welled up in me too, but where could I find my mother’s teat? I was not at all sleepy, so to resist the yearning, I said: "Let me continue, senior monk."

I grew up in a secluded area, with no idea at all of what Manchu and Han meant, and had merely seen the characters for "Manchu-Han Banquet" on restaurant signs without ever feeling curious about them. I often heard people tell stories of "the present dynasty," but tales of literary inquisitions had never reached my ears, and magnificent accounts of the Qianlong Emperor’s Southern Tours were rarely recounted anymore. The most common stories were about "fighting the Long Hairs." There was an elderly maidservant at my home who said she had been in her teens at the time of the Long Hairs, and she told me the most stories about them. But she never drew a moral line between righteousness and evil; she only said there were three kinds of most terrifying things: one, naturally, was the "Long Hairs"; the other, the "Short Hairs"; and the third, the "Flowery Green Heads." It was only later that I realized the latter two were actually imperial troops, yet in the experience of the ignorant common folk, they were no different from the Long Hairs. Rather, it was a few scholars who made clear to me how detestable the Long Hairs were; among the several county histories in my home, I chanced to leaf through them and found that the roster of martyrs—righteous men and chaste women—filled a volume or two. Several members of my own clan were among those killed and were later posthumously bestowed the hereditary title of "Cloud-riding Commandant." Thus I was absolutely convinced of the Long Hairs’ villainy. Yet, indeed it is as they say: "The heart’s affairs like surging waves." As time passed, drawing on my own experience and cross-referencing with the old servant’s accounts, I found it impossible to determine whether the murderers of those martyrs were truly the Long Hairs, or whether they were the "Short Hairs" and the "Flowery Green Heads." How I truly envy the sage’s happiness of "being free of doubts at forty."
