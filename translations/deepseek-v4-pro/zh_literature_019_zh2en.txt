Qin still came often to the Gao family. She visited more frequently, almost every other day. But usually she would go home as soon as it grew dark; if Mrs. Zhang also came to the Gao house to play mahjong, she could stay until the second watch and go back with her mother. Qin spent more time with Shuying, but they also had time to play with Shuhua, Shuzhen, and the others. Sometimes the sisters would arrange to bring Yun over to go boating and have a picnic in the garden. Whenever Yun came, she added more laughter to the group of young girls. Yun learned to row from Shuhua and borrowed translated novels from Shuying. She often laughed innocently, her smile even drawing a smile from the corner of Shuzhen’s lips. Qin, like an elder sister, quietly guided them and looked after them. She did her best to maintain a peaceful, cheerful atmosphere among them. But when they spoke of Hui’s affairs, even Qin would become gloomy and pensive. At such times, Yun’s dimpled smiles would vanish, and she would knit her brows tightly, pouting angrily. At such times, Shuying’s clear eyes would be veiled by dark clouds. They could only express a little sympathy and indignation over Hui’s fate; they could do nothing to ease her suffering. Whenever they spoke of Hui, Qin and the Shuying sisters would long to see her, especially after learning that she had recently recovered from a new illness. They wished she could chat, laugh, and play with them as before. It was not easy to invite Hui to the Gao house. Hui herself had told Juexin that she could not decide for herself. Yet youthful hearts often do not fear difficulties. They tried every way they could, and with the help of Zhou and Juexin, they finally managed to bring Hui over. The day before, when Juexin had given them the good news in advance, how excited and delighted these young girls were.

The weather has turned warm recently, making it suitable for writing. Checking my notes on Song dynasty miscellanea, I find there are still over twenty kinds of ci poetry talks not yet copied out, and even more anecdotes from inscriptions on ancient bronzes and stone tablets remain uncopied; I must concentrate on completing them this year. Poems written before Liberation should also be polished and compiled into a definitive edition this year. I am turning sixty; this task can no longer be delayed. (February 23, 1963, p. 49)

Zhou Bapi struck a full pose to lecture the hired laborers. He said: “I, Zhou Bapi, have no special hobbies in life; I just enjoy watching others bend their backs to work for me. As for what work to do and to what standard it’s done well, you have eyes yourselves—just figure it out. You might ask when to start work and when to knock off—that puts me in a bit of a bind. If I were to give each of you an Omega wristwatch, I might as well go dig in the fields myself. Listen up: you can stop work as soon as it gets dark. By ‘dark’ I don’t mean sunset; it’s when you’re standing together and can’t make out each other’s faces—then you can stop and go home. And when to start? Your little brains are probably thinking: since we stop when it’s dark, we start when it’s light, right? You’re wrong. I, Zhou Bapi, am not that dumb. In winter it gets light late, in summer it gets light early. If it’s cloudy or rainy, at six or seven in the morning it’s still as black as the bottom of a pot. I’m telling you plainly: don’t worry about whether it’s light or dark. Just when you hear the village roosters crow, you can get up and head to the fields...” Having delivered this speech, Zhou Bapi’s figure flickered and he went back to his room to sleep like a log.

In the early Qing, Huang Yuji compiled a catalogue of books from the Ming dynasty, the *Qianqingtang Shumu*. In its 32nd chapter, under collections and the ci-song category, there is a record of the *Yuefu Huncheng Ji* in 105 volumes. It must be the work noted by Zhou Mi and seen by Wang Jide. The 105 volumes tally with Zhou Mi’s mention of “over a hundred massive volumes,” indicating that the printed edition still existed in its entirety during the Ming dynasty. Both Wang and Huang referred to it as the *Huncheng Ji*, so the printed edition must have used that title; thus the variant “Huncheng” (混成) is likely a slip of Zhou Mi’s brush. The proper title of the work should be *Yuefu Huncheng Ji*, with the alternate name *Yuefu Daquan*; “huncheng” carries the sense of “complete compendium.” This work does not appear in Qing bibliographic records, nor has it been cited by anyone; it was probably lost around the fall of the Ming dynasty.
