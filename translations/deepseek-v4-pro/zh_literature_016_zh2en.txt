Amid the rhythmic thud of Zijing chopping filling, Yellow Hair leaned his weary body against the wall and launched into a wild tale for the old woman. "Blind Mother, have you ever heard the story? In Wangge Village, a woman rose early to fetch water from the well and suddenly saw a red lotus bloom as big as a prayer mat floating on the water. Perched on that red lotus was a fair, plump baby. The woman was bewitched out of her senses and plunged headlong into the well—drowned, she was. The lotus baby was a death-demon in disguise," the old woman interjected. "One day, in a downpour, eight bricklayers ran to take shelter in a ruined temple. The thunder and lightning linked up into sheets and balls, and fireballs rolled back and forth at the temple gate. Everyone inside was scared witless. One of them said, 'Among the eight of us, someone must have done something against his conscience. We can't let one rotten rat dropping spoil a whole pot of porridge. Whoever is guilty should go out.' But who would be willing to leave? They pushed and shoved, all tangled up together, unable to sort it out. Another one said, 'All right, all of you, take off your bamboo hats and toss them out the temple door. Whoever's hat gets carried away by the wind, that's the one who goes out to meet his fate.' One man bravely pulled the door open, and wind and rain howled in. One after another, they tossed their hats—each hat blown straight back in, seven in a row, all returned. Only one man remained. Trembling with fear, he picked up his hat and threw it out. A freak gust snatched it and swept it away. The seven said, 'It's you, go out.' Could he be willing to go? The seven didn't argue; they lifted him up and hurled him out." "So what happened? Was he struck dead or not?" "Blind Mother, just listen. After being thrown out, he knelt on the ground, kowtowing like pounding garlic, praying: 'Heavens! O heavens! You must not wrong an innocent man!' Even as he prayed, he heard a great rumble behind him—the entire ruined temple collapsed. The four walls caved inward, the roof crushed down, and not one of the seven escaped. They were wrapped up like a giant meat dumpling!" "Ai-yo, how could such a thing happen!" the old woman exclaimed, amazed. All the gloom brought on by the dreary weather vanished. Just as she was eagerly listening to Yellow Hair's next story, Zijing brought out the steaming dumplings. The old woman's interest still lingered; she insisted that Yellow Hair continue after eating. Zijing set out a bowl of jellyfish and a plate of preserved duck eggs. She pouted at Yellow Hair and said, "There's a bottle of liquor in the back window niche. Have a couple of drinks, take the edge off your fatigue." The old woman said, "Go on, you've been working all day." Yellow Hair grabbed the bottle, bit off the cap, and took three big gulps. The alcohol hit him fast, and his face flushed a bewitching peach-blossom red. Zijing snatched the bottle from him and took a gulp with a loud glug; tears quickly brimmed in her eyes. Yellow Hair's face floated in the curling white steam, a phantom drifting near then far.

A few men were already crawling along the mud path into the cave entrance. Old Zhang was the first. He crawled ahead, lifting his lamp clear of the water’s surface to light the way. But he saw nothing, only the murky dark water all around. The water kept slapping against his face, painfully. He lifted his head to keep from sinking, but his body was nearly trapped by the sludgy mire. With tremendous effort, he crawled forward a few steps. The water surged in, driving him back a step. One careless moment, his hand slipped, and the lamp fell into the water and went dark. His head plunged under; everything before his eyes went black, and a ringing sound filled his ears.

"How can I forget things from the past?" he said, face flushed, struggling with the words. "What matters is that you have a husband of your own choosing, and I have my Zhijun."

"The style is the man"—this saying is not always entirely trustworthy. Sometimes writing is an expression, an opening up; at other times it is a concealment, an evasion, and behind the most heart-rending words there may be hidden things. I have had such experiences myself: often when I most yearn to express myself, I hide a great deal, and I know full well in my heart that what is hidden may be more important than what is expressed. Why is that? Why, knowing it clearly, still hide? Knowing it is important, yet still evade?

"They mean to devour me; you alone can do nothing about it, of course—but why would you join in with them? Those who eat people will stop at nothing; they would eat me, they would eat you, and within their own gang, they might eat one another. Yet if only they would turn about, if only they would change their ways at once, then all would be at peace. Though it has always been so, today we might make an especial effort to do better, but you say it's impossible! Big Brother, I believe you can say so—the day before yesterday when the tenant farmers wanted to reduce the rent, you said it couldn't be done."

Zijin's manner of speaking made Feng Zicun feel extremely ill at ease. Zijin ordinarily said very little, and when he did speak now and then, his words were so evasive they seemed deliberately designed to keep others guessing at his thoughts.

"You don't need to comfort me; I'm not afraid to die. Life has no joy—better to be dead! In death, I'll at least spare the nation a bit of grain," Fourth Uncle said with a smile.
