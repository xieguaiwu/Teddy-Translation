On top of that, there was a sadistic second cook and the stingiest boss imaginable, Zhou Bapi, and I constantly had to hold myself back from spitting at them. Even working just two nights a week dragged on like years, let alone for Boss Li, who spent all day in the shop he once owned. He was old before his time, slow in hand and foot; Zhou Bapi said keeping him on was an act of charity, so there was no question of paying him much. For all these reasons, my hometown friend came looking to chat again. After our shifts we'd go wait for the bus. In the dead of night, we'd wait and wait, sometimes an hour or two, and no bus would come. He swore up and down that the Italian girl had truly been sincere with him at first, and only changed later. Later, the girl said she was going to leave him, but didn't want his money. Besides that, she even found him a wife—a Peruvian woman. There was no telling if she was white, black, or red, because South Americans have the most mixed bloodlines. He didn't understand a word of Spanish, she didn't understand a word of Chinese, and the common language in America, English, was a complete mystery to both of them. There's one thing you can do without talking, so they went ahead and did it, and children came one after another. They were neither quite black nor quite yellow, just grotesque. Strangest of all, every one of those kids spoke only their mother's tongue—not a word of Chinese. Whenever he got home, he'd be surrounded by a silent, staring circle. That atmosphere made his flesh crawl. Only by beating a few of them to tears would he feel a little better inside. He told me that looking at a roomful of brats, he hardly knew what he'd done.

“Ci, you love the sheep, but I love the ritual!”
 This is from the *Analects*, “Bayi”: “Zigong wanted to do away with the sacrificial sheep for the announcement of the new moon. The Master said, ‘Ci, you love the sheep, but I love the ritual!’” According to Zhu Xi’s commentary in the Song dynasty, the sacrificial sheep was a live sheep. The feudal lords would announce the new moon at the ancestral temple on the first day of each month——this was called “announcing the new moon.” Zigong (Duanmu Ci), seeing that the rulers of Lu had already abandoned the ritual of announcing the new moon, wanted to do away with the sheep prepared for the rite as well; but Confucius believed that keeping the sheep could still preserve the form of the ritual, and so he spoke thus.

(1) Ci originated from yuefu, and yuefu originated from poetry. The materials of the four poetic genres, including the Major and Minor *Elegantiae*, amount to one hundred and five pieces. Those materials that possess elegance are what give the *Airs* their beauty and the *Hymns* their fulfillment. From poetry to yuefu to ci, one must aspire to that single word “elegance” if one is to stand out and claim the title of author. Thus Zeng Duanbo selected ci and named it *Elegant Ci of the Yuefu*, and Zhou Gongjin, skilled in ci, inscribed his hall with the motto “Aspire to Elegance.” As a form, ci is sinuous and drawn out; unless woven through with elegance, it rarely fails to drift with the current and lose its proper course. (Preface to *Collected Elegant Ci*)
