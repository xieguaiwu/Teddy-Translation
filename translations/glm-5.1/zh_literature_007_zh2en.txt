"The Five Marquises"
In the second year of the Heping reign of Emperor Cheng of Han (Liu Ao) (27 BC), the five brothers of the imperial consort kin—Wang Tan, Wang Fengshi, Wang Gen, Wang Li, and Wang Shang—were enfeoffed as marquises on the same day. They were referred to at the time as the "Five Marquises." According to the *Biographies of Knight-errants* in the *Book of Han*, the "Five Marquises" kept many Confucian scholars and knights-errant. Among them, the great knight-errant Lou Hu (Junqing) was the most trusted and was known as the "Distinguished Guest of the Five Marquises."

The aroma of millet porridge filled the small room. The woman lifted the lid of the pot. I was surprised to find that there was a lot of porridge in the pot, enough to fill three bowls. The woman brought over three large black bowls from the corner of the room and scooped the porridge into them with a wooden spoon whose rim was scorched. Scoop by scoop by scoop, scoop by scoop by scoop, scoop by scoop by scoop; she filled the three large bowls, yet there was still much left in the pot. I was puzzled, pleasantly surprised, and bewildered. Could all this porridge really have been simmered from those few dozen grains of millet? What kind of person was this woman, exactly? Was she a demon? Was she a goddess? The two foxes that had rushed into the temple during the pouring rain were drawn by the aroma of the porridge and walked boldly into our little room. The vixen was in front, the male fox behind, and toddling between them were three furry little foxes. They were endearingly foolish and very cute. The saying that animals like to give birth during thunderstorms and pouring rain is indeed true. The two large foxes squatted in front of the pot, occasionally looking up at the woman with a pleading gleam in their eyes, and occasionally staring into the pot with a greedy, hungry gleam. Their bellies made rumbling sounds; that was the sound of hunger. The three little foxes rooted under the vixen's belly, searching for her teats. The male fox's eyes were moist and vivid, looking as if he might speak at any moment. I knew that if he were to speak, what he would say. The woman looked at the senior monk. The monk sighed and pushed the large bowl in front of him toward the vixen. The woman followed suit and pushed the bowl of porridge in front of her toward the male fox. After nodding in thanks to the monk and the woman, the two foxes began to eat with loud smacks. The porridge was very hot; they ate carefully, their eyes brimming with tears. I felt very awkward, looking at the porridge before me, not knowing whether I should eat it or not. The senior monk said, "Eat." This was surely the best porridge I had ever eaten, and I would never again eat such good porridge. The two foxes and I each ate three bowls of porridge. The foxes burped with full stomachs, took their little ones, and waddled away. At this point, I noticed that the pot was completely clean; there wasn't even a single grain of millet left. I felt apologetic, but the senior monk had already sat down on the bed, fingering his prayer beads, seemingly falling asleep. The woman sat in front of the coal stove, playing with an iron skewer in her hand. The faint firelight from the stove illuminated her face, making it look so vivid and spirited. She smiled, as if recalling beautiful past memories, or perhaps as if remembering and thinking of nothing at all. I stroked my bulging belly and heard the sound of the little foxes nursing coming from the temple hall outside. I couldn't hear the sound of the kittens nursing in the tree hollow, but I seemed to see them nursing too. I, too, developed a strong desire to nurse, but where was my nipple? I was not sleepy at all. To resist the desire to nurse, I said, "Senior Monk, I will continue speaking."

I grew up in a remote region and had no idea what Manchu and Han meant. I had only seen the words "Manchu-Han Banquet" on restaurant signs, which never prompted any questions. It was common to hear people tell stories about "the present dynasty," but I had never heard of the literary inquisitions, and the grand occasion of Emperor Qianlong's southern tours was rarely recounted. The most frequent topic was "fighting the Long Hairs." There was an elderly maid in my family who said she was already in her teens during the time of the Long Hairs. She was the one who told me the most stories about them, but she made no distinction between good and evil; she only said there were three most terrifying things: one was naturally the "Long Hairs," another was the "Short Hairs," and the third was the "Flower-Green Heads." It was only later that I realized the latter two were actually government troops, but in the experience of the ignorant masses, they were no different from the Long Hairs. It was actually a few scholars who pointed out to me the vileness of the Long Hairs; my family had several local gazetteers, and occasionally when I flipped through them, I found one or two volumes of rosters of martyrs and chaste women who died for the cause. A few people in my clan had also been killed, and later they were granted the hereditary title of "Cloud Cavalryman." Thus, I firmly concluded that the Long Hairs were indeed detestable. However, as the saying goes, "the heart's thoughts are like surging waves." Over time, through my own life experience and corroborated by the old maid's accounts, I actually could no longer determine whether the murderers of those martyrs and chaste women were the Long Hairs, or the "Short Hairs" and "Flower-Green Heads." I truly envied the happiness of the sage who said, "At forty, I had no doubts."
