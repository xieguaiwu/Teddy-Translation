"The Five Marquises"
In the second year of the Heping era (27 BC) of Emperor Cheng of Han (Liu Ao), five brothers from the imperial consort clan—Wang Tan, Wang Fengshi, Wang Gen, Wang Li, and Wang Shang—were enfeoffed as marquises on the same day, and were known at the time as the "Five Marquises." According to the "Biographies of Wandering Knights" in the *Book of Han*, the "Five Marquises" patronized many scholar-knights. Among them, the great knight Lou Hu (courtesy name Junqing) was the most trusted and served as a distinguished guest of the Five Marquises.

The aroma of millet porridge filled the small room. The woman lifted the lid of the pot. To my surprise, there was a great deal of porridge inside, enough to fill three large bowls. She brought over three large black bowls from the corner and began ladling the porridge into them with a wooden spoon whose edges were charred. Scoop after scoop, scoop after scoop, scoop after scoop, she filled the three large bowls, yet there was still plenty left in the pot. I was bewildered, delighted, and utterly confused. Could all this porridge really have been boiled from just a few dozen grains? What kind of person was this woman? Was she a demon? A deity? The two foxes that had dashed into the temple hall during the downpour, drawn by the fragrance of the porridge, now walked unceremoniously into our little room. The vixen led, the dog fox followed, and between them toddled three fluffy little fox cubs. They looked adorably clumsy and utterly charming. It is indeed true that animals prefer to give birth during thunderstorms and torrential rain. The two adult foxes squatted before the pot, sometimes looking up at the woman with eyes gleaming with supplication, and sometimes staring into the pot with eyes gleaming with greed. Their bellies rumbled with the unmistakable sound of hunger. The three cubs nuzzled and pushed beneath their mother's belly, searching for teats. The dog fox's eyes were moist, his gaze so vivid it seemed he was about to speak at any moment. I knew exactly what he would say if he did. The woman looked at the senior monk. The monk sighed and pushed his large bowl in front of the vixen. Following his example, the woman pushed her own bowl of porridge toward the dog fox. After nodding their thanks to the monk and the woman, the two foxes began to eat with loud, rhythmic slurps. The porridge was hot, so they ate cautiously, tears welling in their eyes. I felt awkward, staring at the porridge before me, unsure whether I should eat it or not. "Go ahead and eat," the senior monk said. It was undoubtedly the finest porridge I had ever tasted, and I knew I would never eat such good porridge again. The two foxes and I each ate three bowls. Burping contentedly, the foxes led their cubs away, swaying unsteadily. Only then did I notice that the pot was completely clean, without a single grain of rice left. I felt a pang of regret, but the senior monk was already sitting on the bed, turning his prayer beads, seemingly asleep. The woman sat before the coal-briquette stove, idly playing with an iron poker. The faint glow of the fire illuminated her face, making it look so vivid and animated. She smiled, as if recalling beautiful memories, or perhaps remembering and thinking of nothing at all. Rubbing my full belly, I heard the sound of the fox cubs nursing from the temple hall outside. I could not hear the sound of kittens nursing in the tree hollow, but I could almost see them doing the same. A strong desire to nurse myself arose within me, but where were my teats? I felt not the slightest bit sleepy. To resist the urge to nurse, I said, "Senior monk, I will continue speaking."

I grew up in a remote area and knew nothing of the distinction between Manchus and Han Chinese. I had only ever seen the words "Manchu-Han Banquet" on restaurant signs, and it never aroused any questions in my mind. It was common to hear people tell stories of "the present dynasty," but I had never heard of literary inquisitions. The grand events of Emperor Qianlong's southern tours[376] were rarely recounted either. What I heard most often were tales of "fighting the Long Hairs." An elderly female servant in our household said she was already in her teens when the Long Hairs came. She told me more Long Hair stories than anyone else, but she made no distinction between right and wrong. She simply said there were three most terrifying kinds of creatures: one was naturally the "Long Hairs," another the "Short Hairs," and the third the "Flower-Green Heads"[377]. It was only later that I realized the latter two were actually government troops, but in the experience of the ignorant common folk, they were no different from the Long Hairs. It was actually a few scholars who pointed out to me how detestable the Long Hairs were. We had several county gazetteers at home, and when I happened to flip through them, I found one or two volumes listing the names of martyrs and chaste women who had died for the cause. Several members of my own clan had also been killed, and were later posthumously granted the title of "Hereditary Cloud Cavalry Commandant"[378]. Thus, I firmly concluded that the Long Hairs were indeed vile. Yet, as the saying goes, "the mind is like surging waves"[379]. Over time, drawing on my own experiences and corroborating them with the servant's accounts, I found myself unable to decide whether the murderers of those martyrs and chaste women were actually the Long Hairs, or the "Short Hairs" and "Flower-Green Heads." I truly envied the bliss of the sage who "at forty had no doubts"[380].
