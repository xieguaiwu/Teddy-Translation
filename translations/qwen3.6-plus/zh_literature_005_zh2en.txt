I’ve only been working at the × Kitchen for two months, but it feels like years. It’s the endless washing of plates and bowls, taking out the trash. On top of that, there’s a sadistic sous-chef and a viciously stingy boss, Zhou Bapi, that I constantly have to fight the urge to spit on. I only work two nights a week, yet each day drags like a year; I can’t imagine how it must be for Boss Li, who spends all day in the shop he used to own. He’s aged prematurely, his hands and feet slow. Zhou Bapi says taking him in is an act of charity, so he can’t pay him much. For all these reasons, my fellow townsman came to chat with me again. After our shifts, we’d go wait for the bus. In the dead of night, we’d wait an hour or two with no bus in sight. He swore that the Italian girl had truly loved him at first, but later changed. Eventually, she said she was leaving him, but didn’t want his money. Besides that, she even found him a wife—a Peruvian woman. You couldn’t really call her white, black, or red, since South Americans have the most mixed bloodlines. He didn’t understand Spanish, she didn’t understand Chinese, and neither knew a word of English, the common language of America. But there’s one thing you can do without talking, so they did it, and children came one after another. Each was neither quite black nor quite yellow, looking almost grotesque. Another strange thing: all the kids spoke their mother’s language, not a single word of Chinese. Whenever he came home, he was met with a silent, staring circle. The atmosphere was chilling. Only after beating a few of them to tears could he feel a bit better. He told me that looking at a house full of little brats, he had no idea what he’d even done.

Arishima Takeo originally studied agriculture, but while doing so, he also researched literature, eventually devoting himself entirely to literary pursuits. His *Collected Works* were published in installments during his lifetime; *Rebels* is the fourth volume, containing studies of three literary figures[370]; what is translated and printed here is the first essay.

One thing must be made clear: I’ve been working hard at technical jobs for nearly a decade, yet my skills have not improved at all; in fact, I’d say I’ve grown clumsier with time. The situation around us has only worsened, and with my dull mind, I can’t think of any solution. Look at my colleagues—they’re just like me. If anyone seems relatively smart or promising, they end up developing number blindness. Only fools like me are spared from it.

A girl’s voice settles at sixteen. For over a decade after that, her voice has hovered in my ears almost every day. This overly familiar sound has swept away all my passion. So now, in this twilight, as I look at my wife sitting across from me, I feel only a growing exhaustion. She is still knitting that sky-blue scarf. Her face is still the same face it used to be. Only now, it has lost its former youthful firmness. The wrinkles on her face have formed under my gaze; I know them as well as I know the palms of my own hands. Now, she has begun to pay attention to my words.

“Ci, you care for the sheep; I care for the ritual!”
This quote comes from the *Analects*, “Ba Yi” chapter: “Zigong wished to do away with the sacrificial sheep used for the monthly announcement of the new moon. The Master said, ‘Ci, you care for the sheep; I care for the ritual!’” According to the Song dynasty commentator Zhu Xi: *xi yang* refers to a live sheep. On the first day of each lunar month, feudal lords would report to the ancestral temple and attend to state affairs, a ceremony known as *gao shuo*. Zigong (Duanmu Ci), seeing that the ruler of Lu had already abandoned the *gao shuo* ritual, wanted to eliminate the sheep prepared for it as well. But Confucius believed that as long as the sheep remained, a trace of the ritual’s form could be preserved, hence his remark.

(1) *Ci* poetry originates from *yuefu*, and *yuefu* originates from *shi* poetry. The poems of the Greater and Lesser Odes in the *Classic of Poetry* total one hundred and five. It is the refined nature of these materials that accounts for the beauty of the *Airs* and the solemnity of the *Hymns*. In the evolution from *shi* to *yuefu* to *ci*, one must aspire to the single ideal of “elegance” to stand apart and rightfully claim the title of a true author. Thus, Zeng Duanbo named his selected *ci* collection *Yuefu Yaci* (Elegant *Ci* from the Music Bureau), and Zhou Gongjin, a master of *ci*, inscribed his study “Zhi Ya” (Aspiring to Elegance). As a literary form, *ci* is intricate and leisurely; unless interwoven with elegance, few works can avoid being swept away by vulgar trends and losing their proper character. When I returned home for the Mid-Autumn Festival the year before last, I heard that Sixth Aunt had fallen into depression. I went to Sixth Uncle’s house to look at paintings, and sure enough, it was true. While others with depression tend to withdraw into silence, Sixth Aunt talked nonstop, pouring out every grievance of her life; every single one of those grievances was tied to Sixth Uncle. Sixth Uncle kept his head down, saying nothing, merely pointing at the paintings and gazing at them. Amid her endless chatter, how could I possibly focus on the art? After glancing at two or three pieces, I made an excuse about having guests at home for lunch and left Sixth Uncle’s house.
