Qin still visited the Gao family frequently. In fact, she came even more often now, almost every other day. However, she usually went home as soon as it grew dark; only if Mrs. Zhang also came to the Gao house to play cards would she stay until the second watch of the night and return home with her mother. Qin spent most of her time with Shuying, but they also found moments to play with Shuhua and Shuzhen. Sometimes the sisters would even arrange to bring Yun over to row boats and have picnics in the garden. Whenever Yun arrived, she brought even more laughter to the group of young girls. Yun learned to row from Shuhua and borrowed translated novels from Shuying. She often smiled with innocent delight, and her smiles even coaxed a faint smile onto Shuzhen’s lips. Like an elder sister, Qin quietly guided and cared for them, striving to maintain an atmosphere of peace and joy among them. Yet whenever they spoke of Hui, even Qin would fall into a gloomy silence. At such times, Yun’s dimples would vanish, her brows would knit tightly, and she would pout in anger. At such times, a shadow would cloud Shuying’s clear eyes. All they could do for Hui’s fate was express a little sympathy and indignation, utterly powerless to ease her suffering by even a fraction. Whenever Hui was mentioned, Qin and the Shu sisters longed to see her, especially after learning she had recently recovered from a new illness. They hoped she could once again join them in conversation and play as she used to. Inviting Hui to the Gao house was no easy matter. Hui herself had told Juexin that she could not make her own decisions. Yet the hearts of the young are rarely daunted by difficulties. After exhausting every possible method and enlisting the help of Madam Zhou and Juexin, they finally succeeded in bringing Hui over. When Juexin broke the good news to them the day before, how thrilled and overjoyed the young girls were!

The weather has grown warmer in recent days, making it just tolerable for writing. Upon reviewing the Song dynasty notebooks, I find that over twenty types of ci poetry discourses remain uncopied, and there are even more uncopied records of epigraphic relics. I must devote all my efforts to completing them this year. The poems I wrote before liberation should also be polished and compiled into a definitive edition this year. Having reached the age of sixty, this task can no longer be delayed. (February 23, 1963, p. 49)

Zhou Bapi struck a commanding pose and lectured his long-term farmhands. He said: "I, Zhou Bapi, have no special vices in life. I simply enjoy watching others bend their backs to work for me. As for what work to do and how much is enough, you all have eyes; figure it out yourselves. If you're going to ask when to start work and when to finish, that really puts me in a bind. If I were to give each of you an Omega watch, I might as well go down to the fields and work myself. Listen up: you can stop work when it gets dark. And by 'dark,' I don't mean when the sun sets. I mean when you stand together and can no longer see each other's faces clearly, that's when you can head home. Now, what about starting work? Your little brains might be thinking: if finishing is at dark, then starting must be at dawn, right? You're wrong. I, Zhou Bapi, am not that foolish. In winter, dawn comes late; in summer, it comes early. And if it's cloudy or raining, by six or seven in the morning, the sky is still as black as the bottom of a pot. Let me tell you straight: don't worry about whether it's light or dark. As soon as you hear the roosters crow in the village, get up and head to the fields..." Having finished his speech, Zhou Bapi's figure flickered, and he turned back into his house to sleep soundly.

In the early Qing dynasty, Huang Yujin compiled a catalog of Ming-dynasty works entitled *Qianqing Tang Shumu* (Catalog of the Qianqing Hall). In Volume 32, under the "Ci and Qu" category of the "Collected Works" section, it lists *Yuefu Huncheng Ji* in 105 volumes, which must be the work recorded by Zhou Mi and seen by Wang Jide. The count of 105 volumes also aligns with Zhou Mi’s description of "over a hundred large fascicles," indicating that the complete printed edition of this book still existed in the Ming dynasty. Both Wang and Huang refer to it as *Huncheng Ji*, which must reflect the title on the printed edition; therefore, the variant "Huncheng" (written with a different character) is likely a slip of the pen by Zhou Mi. The full proper title of the work should be *Yuefu Huncheng Ji*, with the alternative title *Yuefu Daquan* ("Complete Collection of Yuefu"), as "huncheng" carries the same meaning as "daquan" (complete/comprehensive). This book does not appear in Qing dynasty bibliographic records, nor has it ever been cited by anyone; it was most likely lost during the transition between the Ming and Qing dynasties.
