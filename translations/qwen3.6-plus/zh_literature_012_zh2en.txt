Although I am not very old, I know quite a few people who have taken their own lives. From what I recall, the leadership often harbors more hatred for the dead than for the living, heaping them with a litany of charges—severing ties with the higher authorities, severing ties with the people, earning eternal infamy, and so on. But these charges hardly frighten the dead. Regardless, they leave the leadership with a major dilemma: in such a wonderful life, how could those heartless wretches bear to abandon it. Take Hong Fu, for example. If she truly sought death out of grief for her husband, that would be forgivable. The real fear is that she might not have meant what she said. If that were the case, one would have to question her clearly before she was completely dead. But that story will have to wait until later. What I want to talk about now is how Hong Fu created misunderstandings in Chang'an. These tales sound quite captivating when I tell them, because my greatest specialty is also creating misunderstandings.

Jianyun was deeply moved. Such a look and such words had completely conquered his heart. Deeply touched, even with a sense of reverence, he called out, "Second Miss," and then said, choking up, "I will never forget your words. No one has ever said such things to me, no one has ever—"

"Grandpa, you were talking about singing. In my dream last night, I heard a wonderfully beautiful song, so soft and lingering. It felt as though I followed the sound, flying all over, until I reached halfway up the cliff across the stream. I picked a large handful of tiger-ear grass. But now that I have it, I don't know who to give it to. I slept so soundly, and the dream was so lovely!"

That same afternoon, the conference presidium notified the groups to hold discussions, and the attendants delivered the three newspapers subscribed for the delegates during the conference. Those who were speaking continued, while those who hadn't yet spoken opened their newspapers. Zhuang Zhidie first read the arts and culture section on page three of the provincial newspaper, then looked at the municipal paper. Almost all of pages one and two were various reports about the conference, which he found rather dull, so he turned to the third paper, called *Weekend*, and was immediately drawn to a news item. The headline read: "Sluggish Start at Municipal Government Compound; Over Half Arrive After Half an Hour." The content turned out to be a sudden survey conducted by the paper's reporter at the municipal government gate on the morning of [Month] [Date]: how many people arrived ten minutes after work started, how many after twenty minutes, and how many after half an hour. How many bureau directors were late, and how many deputy mayors were late. Immediately, the meeting erupted in discussion, and the topic shifted from debating the mayor's government work report to arguing over this news report. Zhuang Zhidie listened for a while; it was nothing but a chaotic chorus of complaints. Finding it utterly tedious, he returned to his room and called home to ask if there was anything urgent. Liu Yue answered, repeatedly asking, "Who is it? Who is it?" Just as Zhuang was about to speak, playful banter came through the phone. Wanting to hear who was playing around, he stayed silent. Liu Yue said on the other end, "Crazy!" and clicked the receiver down. Zhuang dialed again. Without bothering to ask who it was, Liu Yue shouted, "Wrong number, this is a crematorium!" and hung up again. Furious, Zhuang dialed a third time. The moment someone picked up, he scolded, "Liu Yue, is this how you answer the phone at home?!" Recognizing his voice, Liu Yue quickly said, "Teacher Zhuang, how is it you? You've been away these past few days, and dozens of calls come looking for you every day. I told them you weren't here, but the calls kept coming. The eldest sister told me to just answer and say it's the wrong number. I never thought I'd accidentally cut off your call." Zhuang was still angry: "Who was talking with you just now?" Liu Yue said, "It's Hong Jiang. He just came looking for you. Do you want to speak with him?" Hong Jiang's voice came on the line. At first he was hesitant and unclear, but when the topic turned to the bookstore, he immediately said that the manuscript had been printed two days ago, distributed to retail outlets across various regions, and was selling extremely well. Hong Jiang babbled on for a long time while Zhuang remained silent. Finally, Hong Jiang asked, "Teacher Zhuang, are you listening?" Zhuang said, "Mm." Hong Jiang said, "We've really hit the jackpot this time. I've roughly calculated it: with an investment of a hundred thousand, we can net thirty thousand! Given the current market, I'm thinking we should print another ten thousand copies in ten days or half a month. So I was wondering if we should treat that guy surnamed Jia from the postal distribution department? We really can't afford to offend him. Besides the official distribution channels, he holds an underground distribution network. If you think this is a good idea, could you meet him in person? Tomorrow, or the day after?" Zhuang said, "I don't have time. Talk to your teacher's wife about it." He hung up, smoothed out the bed, and slept straight through until dinnertime.

The mother was stunned, her heavy, large head drooping sorrowfully. Suddenly, she burst into loud sobs. Weeping, she went into the room. Sima Ting sighed, squatting under the pear tree to smoke.

Perhaps every birth of a webbed-footed baby marked the beginning of a tragic era in the family's history; otherwise, why would Grandfather be so terrified? His face was deathly pale, and the scorched-yellow beard on his chin curled and trembled like cogon grass roots in a flame, trembling and curling. His tall frame swayed unsteadily, as if he might collapse at any moment and crumble into a heap of debris.
