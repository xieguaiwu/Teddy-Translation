Amid the rhythmic *ding-dong* of Zijing chopping the filling, Huang Mao leaned his weary body against the wall and began spinning tales from far and wide for the old woman. "Blind Mother, have you ever heard of a woman in Wanggezhuang who went to draw water early one morning and suddenly saw a red lotus flower, as big as a meditation cushion, floating in the well? The lotus was cradling a chubby, fair-skinned baby. Bewitched out of her wits, she plunged headfirst into the well and drowned—" "The lotus baby was a death-summoning spirit in disguise," the old woman interjected. "—One day, during a heavy downpour, eight bricklayers took shelter in a dilapidated temple. The thunder and lightning merged into a single sheet, forming a ball of fire that rolled back and forth before the temple door. Everyone inside was scared out of their wits. One of them said, 'Among the eight of us, someone must have done something wicked. We can't let one rotten apple spoil the barrel. Whoever is guilty should go out.' But who would volunteer? So they pushed and shoved, tangling into a chaotic mess. Another suggested, 'Let's all take off our bamboo hats and throw them out the temple door. Whoever's hat gets blown away by the wind will go out to face death.' One man bravely pulled the temple door open, and wind and rain came rushing in with a howl. They took turns throwing their hats out; each one was blown right back in. They threw seven, and all seven returned. Only one man was left. Trembling, he picked up his hat and tossed it out. A wicked gust of wind snatched it away. The other seven said, 'Looks like it's you. Go out.' How could he refuse? Without another word, the seven lifted him up and threw him out—" "What happened then? Was he struck dead by lightning?" "Blind Mother, listen to me. After being thrown out, the man fell to his knees, kowtowing like a pestle pounding garlic, praying, 'Heaven, oh Heaven, please don't wrong an innocent man!' As he prayed, a tremendous crash echoed behind him. The entire dilapidated temple collapsed, its four walls caving inward and the roof crashing down. Not a single one of the seven escaped; they were all sealed inside like a giant dumpling stuffed with human flesh—" "Oh my, to think such things could happen!" the old woman exclaimed repeatedly. All her gloom from the dreary weather vanished. Just as she was eagerly waiting for Huang Mao to start his next tale, Zijing brought out a steaming bowl of dumplings. The old woman, still in high spirits, insisted that Huang Mao continue his stories after the meal. Zijing set down a bowl of shredded jellyfish and a plate of century eggs, then pursed her lips at Huang Mao and said, "There's a bottle of wine in the rear window niche. Have a couple of swigs to ease your fatigue." The old woman added, "Drink up. You've worked hard all day." Huang Mao took the bottle, bit off the cap, and downed three big gulps. The alcohol quickly took effect, bringing a peach-blossom flush to his cheeks. Zijing snatched the bottle from his hands, took a loud gulp herself, and her eyes instantly filled with tears. Huang Mao's face floated in the curling white steam, appearing and disappearing like a phantom.

Several people had already crawled into the tunnel opening along the narrow passage. Old Zhang was the first. He crawled ahead, holding the lamp above the water to light the way. But he could see nothing except the expanse of murky, dark water. The water frequently splashed against his face, stinging sharply. He kept his head raised to avoid sinking, but his body was nearly swallowed by the thick mud. Summoning all his strength, he crawled forward a few steps. A surge of water from ahead struck him, pushing him back a step. In a moment of carelessness, his grip slipped, and the lamp fell into the water and went out. His head plunged underwater, darkness swam before his eyes, and a ringing sound filled his ears.

"How could I possibly forget the past?" he said, struggling as his face flushed red. "The most important thing is that you have the husband you chose for yourself, and I have my Zhijun."

Behind tear-jerking words, there may well be hidden depths. I have had such experiences myself: often, when I longed to express myself, I ended up concealing much, fully aware that what was hidden might be even more important than what was revealed. Why is this? Why conceal even when one knows it clearly? Why evade what one knows to be important?
