“The Five Marquises”
In the second year of the Heping reign (27 BCE) of Emperor Cheng of the Han (Liu Ao), five brothers of the imperial in-laws—Wang Tan, Wang Fengshi, Wang Gen, Wang Li, and Wang Shang—were enfeoffed as marquises on the same day. They were known at the time as the “Five Marquises.” According to the “Biographies of the Wandering Knights” in the *Book of Han*, the “Five Marquises” kept many Confucian scholars and knights-errant, among whom the great knight Lou Hu (Junqing) was the most trusted and esteemed, being the “honored guest of the Five Marquises.”

The aroma of millet porridge filled the small room. The woman lifted the pot lid. I was astonished to discover that there was a great deal of porridge in the pot, enough to fill three large bowls. The woman fetched three big black bowls from the corner and ladled porridge into them with a wooden spoon whose edges were charred. Spoonful after spoonful, spoonful after spoonful, spoonful after spoonful, she filled the three bowls, yet there was still much left in the pot. I was puzzled, delighted, and utterly confused. Could this abundance of porridge have been cooked from those few dozen grains? What kind of person was this woman? Was she a demon? Was she an immortal? The two foxes that had dashed into the temple during the downpour were drawn by the aroma of the porridge and boldly entered our small room. The vixen led the way, the dog fox followed behind, and between them toddled three fluffy cubs. They were endearingly silly and utterly adorable. It is said that animals like to give birth when thunder crashes and rain pours down—how true that is! The two large foxes squatted before the pot, now looking up at the woman with pleading glints in their eyes, now staring at the pot with greedy glints in their eyes. Their stomachs rumbled—sounds of hunger. The three cubs nosed about under the vixen’s belly, searching for teats. The dog fox’s eyes were wet, his gaze vivid and animated, as though he might open his mouth to speak at any moment. I knew what he would say if he opened his mouth. The woman looked at the Great Monk. The Great Monk sighed and pushed the large bowl before him toward the vixen. The woman followed suit, pushing her bowl of porridge toward the dog fox. After nodding their thanks to the Great Monk and the woman, the two foxes began to eat noisily. The porridge was hot; they ate cautiously, tears in their eyes. I was embarrassed, looking at the porridge before me, unsure whether I should eat or not. The Great Monk said: Eat. This was surely the best porridge I had ever eaten; I would never eat such good porridge again. The foxes and I each ate three bowls. The foxes burped, gathered their cubs, and staggered away. And then I noticed that the pot was completely clean, not a single grain of rice left. I felt apologetic, but the Great Monk was already sitting on the bed, fingering his prayer beads, as if asleep. The woman sat before the briquette stove, toying with an iron skewer. The feeble firelight illuminated her face, making it so vivid and spirited. She smiled, as though recalling happy memories, or as though recalling nothing and thinking nothing. I stroked my bulging belly and heard, from the temple hall outside, the sound of the cubs nursing. I could not hear the kittens nursing in the tree hollow, but I seemed to see them nursing too. I too felt a strong desire to nurse, but where were my teats? I had no sleepiness whatsoever. To resist the desire to nurse, I said: Great Monk, I will continue.

I grew up in a remote area and had not the slightest idea what Manchu and Han meant; I had only seen the words “Manchu-Han Banquet” on restaurant signs, which never gave rise to any questions in me. It was common to hear people tell stories of “the present dynasty,” but I had never heard of literary inquisitions, and the grand occasion of the Qianlong Emperor’s southern tour[376] was seldom spoken of. What was talked about most was “fighting the Long Hairs.” There was an old maidservant in my family who said she was already in her teens during the time of the Long Hairs; she told me more stories about them than anyone else, but she made no distinction between good and evil. She only said there were three most terrifying things: naturally, one was the “Long Hairs,” another was the “Short Hairs,” and the third was the “Flower-Green Heads.”[377] Later, I came to understand that the latter two were actually government troops, but in the experience of the ignorant populace, they were no different from the Long Hairs. It was a few literati who pointed out to me how detestable the Long Hairs were; my family had several volumes of county gazetteers, and when I happened to open them, I found one or two volumes listing the names of martyred men and women from that time. Several people of my own clan had been killed and were later enfeoffed as “hereditary cloud cavalry commanders,”[378] so I then firmly decided that the Long Hairs were detestable. Yet, as the saying goes, “the heart’s affairs are like surging waves”;[379] as time went by, through my own experience and corroborated by the maidservant’s tales, I could no longer determine whether the murderers of those martyred men and women were the Long Hairs or the “Short Hairs” and “Flower-Green Heads.” I truly envy the happiness of the sage who “at forty is free from perplexity.”[380]
