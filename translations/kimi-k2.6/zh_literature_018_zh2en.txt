Father rubbed his eyes with his palm and said, “Get the hell out! Take your ox-dad and ox-mom and get out of here, don’t let these animals foul my courtyard.” Mother said, “Guoguo, even a fierce tiger doesn’t eat its own young. Your father and I are only thinking of your own good. Listen, send these stinky oxen back, and let’s live a proper life.” Father said, “A grown son doesn’t listen to his father. Go ahead and make your trouble. Without some debt between us, we’d never have been father and son.”

They looked at each other, then burst into peals of laughter. I stood there watching them laugh, and laughed too. People crossing the bridge saw us laughing so loudly, and they all burst into hearty laughter as well. A man in a floral shirt called out to me:

“Let me introduce you—this is my wife, her name is Wan Zhaohua, you can just call her Zhaohua from now on; this is Old Li, the Old Li I often talk about.” My friend introduced us with evident pride. His wife smiled faintly and nodded gently. I lowered my head so low it seemed I was bowing. When I raised my head, I heard her say: “I often hear him speak of Mr. Li. Mr. Li is living here, and we are poor hosts; I’m afraid there may be places where we have been neglectful…”

Father parted the sorghum stalks and darted swiftly toward the northwest, toward our village. A badger scurried clumsily along the sorghum furrows, but Father paid it no mind. Once he reached the dirt road, free of the sorghum’s hindrance, he ran as fast as a wild hare. The heavy Browning pistol dragged his red cloth belt into a crescent. The pistol knocked against his hip bones, and in that stinging, numbing pain, Father felt he had become a true man who raises his sword and leaps upon his horse. The village came into view in the distance, and the hundred-year-old ginkgo tree at the village head, lush and green, solemnly greeted Father. Father drew the gun and held it in his hand, running while taking aim at the elegant bird silhouettes gliding across the sky.

Father said, “What are you executing me for? Blame your own poor marksmanship, not me.” He sharply criticized the company commander and the political instructor’s shooting technique, just like a squad leader criticizing two soldiers. He said the instructor was crippled in his right hand, so it was understandable he couldn’t shoot straight with his left—but you, Commander, are missing not a single finger from either hand, yet you aim at a crow and hit a donkey. What kind of sense does that make? What are you laughing at? It turned out the commander had an extra finger on his left hand. With eleven fingers and you still can’t shoot straight, and you have the nerve to curse me? Watch me show you how it’s done. As he spoke, he took the gun from the commander’s hand, his movements casual and natural, without the slightest affectation. The commander showed no reluctance, and no one else felt anything awkward about it. Father pulled open the commander’s gun chamber, looked at it against the sunlight, then felt the muzzle, and said with contempt: “This old, worn-out junk—nobody would pick it up if you threw it in the street. Back in the day, my old man’s German mirror-finish, now that was quality. One pull of the trigger and it cackled like a young rooster—that was a gun!” As he spoke, he snatched the instructor’s gun too. The instructor let out a strange cry, and a fit of violent coughing bent him double.

By the well, the tung tree is newly budding; in the boudoir, the grass is fragrant. A small scroll, a portrait of Shuangwen; a sheer silk skirt with a hundred pleats. I had only meant to follow the drifting willow catkins in a dream, who knew the moon would fall into heavy clouds. I hide the fact that I have always been thin; this frailty is not for your sake.

In 1970, I was nine years old. I heard that a steel roller mill had been installed in the neighboring village, powered by a diesel engine that spun it round. Once the belt was hooked up, it hummed and buzzed, grinding several hundred pounds of wheat in an hour. Many families in our village had torn out their stone millstones; if they wanted flour, they took money and went to grind it at the steel mill. Our family’s stone mill hadn’t been torn out yet—we had no money.

Genzhu thought for a moment: “I also think the matter should be settled, but these past two weeks, several girls from Ding Village who died of the fever have been married off to dead men from other villages. Dig up the grave, arrange the ghost marriage, and the female corpses of our Ding Village are carried away. My cousin Hongli had already arranged a ghost marriage with Cuizi, the niece of Zhao Xiuqin, but just yesterday, they promised Cuizi to the Ma family of Willow Village. They say Ding Hui brokered this ghost marriage. They say he took a hundred-yuan ghost-marriage fee from each side. They say the Ma family gave Cuizi’s family three thousand yuan in betrothal gifts.” At this point, Genzhu rested his gaze on Grandpa’s face again, and added emphasis as he continued: “It’s not just me who wants your Ding Hui dead. Many people in the village aren’t willing to let him go on living in this world.”

Wuchengzi
No longer extant. Also, in the Five Elements category, Wuchengzi’s Responses to Disasters and Anomalies is listed in fourteen volumes; in the Bedchamber Arts category, Wuchengzi’s The Yin Way is listed in thirty-six volumes. Both are lost. Wucheng is a compound surname; the given name is Zhao, though some say it is Fu. The Eastern Han work Qianfu Lun (Comments of a Recluse), in its “Zanxue” (In Praise of Learning) chapter, records that “Yao took Wucheng as his teacher.”
