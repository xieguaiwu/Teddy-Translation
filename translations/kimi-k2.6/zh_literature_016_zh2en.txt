To the rhythmic sound of Zijin chopping the filling, Huangmao leaned his weary body against the wall and began telling the old woman stories from far and wide. “Blind Mother, have you ever heard that in Wangge Village, a woman went to draw water one morning and suddenly saw a red lotus as big as a rush cushion in the well? On the lotus sat a plump, white baby. The woman lost her senses and plunged headfirst into the well—she drowned.” — “The lotus baby was a death-fetching demon in disguise,” the old woman said. — “One day during a heavy downpour, eight masons ran into a dilapidated temple to take shelter from the rain. The thunder and lightning joined in sheets and formed into balls; fireballs rolled back and forth before the temple gate. The people inside were scared out of their wits. One of them said, ‘Among us eight, someone must have done something against his conscience. We can’t let one rotten mouse dropping spoil the whole pot of porridge. Whoever is guilty should go out.’ But who was willing to go out? So they pushed one another, you pushing me, me pushing you, tangled in a confused mess. Then another said, ‘Let’s do this: everyone take off your bamboo hats and throw them out the temple door. Whosever hat is blown away by the wind must go out to die.’ Someone boldly pulled open the temple door, and the wind and rain rushed in with a howl. They took turns throwing out their hats; every one thrown was blown back—all seven of them. Only one person was left. Trembling, he picked up his hat and threw it out. A gust of evil wind swept the hat away. The seven others said, ‘It’s you. Go out.’ How could he be willing? The seven men gave him no chance to object, lifted him up, and threw him out.” — “Well then? Was that person struck dead?” — “Blind Mother, listen to me. After being thrown out, the man knelt on the ground, kowtowing like pounding garlic, praying: ‘Heaven, oh Heaven, you must not wrong an innocent man!’ As he was praying, he heard a thunderous crash behind him. The entire dilapidated temple collapsed; the four walls caved inward, the roof pressed down, and none of the seven men inside escaped—they were wrapped into a giant human-filled dumpling.” — “Oh my, how could such a thing happen!” The old woman exclaimed repeatedly. The gloomy weather had brought her displeasure, but now it vanished completely. Just as she was listening with great interest to Huangmao’s next story, Zijin brought out steaming hot dumplings. The old woman was still in high spirits and made Huangmao promise to continue after the meal. Zijin brought over a bowl of shredded jellyfish and a plate of preserved eggs, pursed her lips at Huangmao and said: “There’s a bottle of wine in the back window opening. Have a couple of mouthfuls to relieve your fatigue.” The old woman said: “Have some. You’ve been working hard all day.” Huangmao took the wine, bit off the cap, and drank three big gulps in succession. The alcohol quickly took effect; his face flushed with a peach-blossom redness. Zijin snatched the bottle from his hand, gulped down a mouthful, and tears instantly filled her eyes. Huangmao’s face floated in the curling white steam, like a phantom now distant, now near.

Several people had already crawled into the tunnel opening along the winding path. Old Zhang was the first. He crawled in front, holding the lamp above the water to light the way. But he could see nothing, only that expanse of dark water. The water constantly struck his face, stinging painfully. He raised his head to avoid sinking into the water; his body was almost trapped by the mud. He exerted great effort to crawl forward a few steps. The water rushing from ahead knocked him back a step. Carelessly, his hand loosened, and the lamp fell into the water and went out. His head sank into the water; a sudden darkness passed before his eyes, and a ringing sound echoed in his ears.

“How could I forget the past?” he said, his face red, struggling. “The most important thing is that you have a husband of your own choosing, and I have my Zhijun.”

“The writing mirrors the man” is not absolutely trustworthy. Writing is sometimes expression, an opening up; sometimes it is concealment, an evasion. Behind moving, tear-jerking words there may very well be hidden things. I myself have had such experiences: often when longing to express, I instead did much hiding, and clearly knew in my heart that what was hidden might be more important than what was expressed. Why is this? Why, knowing clearly in one’s heart, must one still hide? Knowing that something is important, yet still evading it?

“They want to eat me. You alone, of course, can do nothing about it; but why join the gang? Those who eat people will stop at nothing; they will eat me, they will eat you too, and within the gang they will also eat each other. But only take one step, only change immediately, and then everyone will be at peace. Though it has always been so, today we can be especially good, and say it cannot be done! Elder Brother, I believe you can say it—the day before yesterday, when the tenant wanted to reduce the rent, you said it cannot be done.”

Zijin’s manner of speaking made Feng Zicun feel very uncomfortable. He usually spoke very little, and even when he occasionally said a word or two, it was equivocal, as if deliberately making it impossible for others to fathom his thoughts.
