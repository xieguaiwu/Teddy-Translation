I worked at the × kitchen for two months, yet it felt like several years. Because there was no end to washing dishes, scrubbing plates, and hauling out the trash. Besides that, there was a sadistic sous-chef, and a boss, Zhou the Skinflint, who was unspeakably mean—I was forever fighting the urge to spit in their faces. I only worked two nights a week and every day felt like a year; how much worse was it for Boss Li, who spent all day in the shop he once owned? He was prematurely aged, slow in his movements; Zhou the Skinflint said that taking him in was an act of charity, so he could not be paid much. For all these reasons, my fellow townsman came to talk to me again. After work we would go to wait for the bus. In the dead of night, we would wait an hour or two for a bus that never came. He swore that the Italian girl had truly loved him at first, and only changed later. Later the girl said she was leaving him, but she did not want his money. Besides that, she found him a wife, a Peruvian woman. You could not say whether this woman was white, black, or red; South American blood is the most mixed. He did not understand Spanish, she did not understand Chinese, and neither of them had the slightest grasp of English, the common language of America. There was one thing you could do without speaking, so they did it, and the children were born one after another. Each one was neither black nor yellow—utterly grotesque in appearance. There was another odd thing: all the children spoke their mother’s language, not a word of Chinese. The moment he got home, he would find himself surrounded by a circle of silent onlookers. That atmosphere made one’s hair stand on end. Only by beating a few of them until they cried could he feel a little better. He told me that looking at the roomful of little brats, he hardly knew what he had done.

Arishima Takeo studied agriculture, but he also studied literature and art, and later devoted himself entirely to literature and art. His *Collected Works* were published in successive volumes during his lifetime; *The Rebel* was the fourth volume, containing studies of three literary artists [370]; what is translated and printed here is the first of them.

There is one thing that must be made clear: I have worked hard at technical work for nearly ten years, but my skill level has not advanced at all; one could even say I have become more and more stupid. The situation around us has also grown worse and worse; with my dull brain I can think of no solution. Looking at my colleagues, they are the same as me. If anyone seems relatively clever, with a promising future, they come down with number-blindness. Only fools like me are spared from number-blindness.

A girl’s voice is fixed by the age of sixteen. For the more than ten years that followed, her voice circled in my ears almost every day. This overly familiar voice had already swept away my passion. So at this moment, in the dusk, looking at my wife sitting across from me, I only feel increasingly tired. She is still knitting that sky-blue scarf. Her face is still the face of the past. Only the face of this moment has lost the elasticity of former days. The wrinkles on her face grew up beneath my gaze; I know them as well as I know the back of my own hand. Now she begins to pay attention to my words.

“Ci, you love the sheep, but I love the ritual!”
From the *Analects*, “Ba Yi”: “Zi Gong wished to do away with the sacrificial sheep for the announcement of the new moon. The Master said, ‘Ci, you love the sheep, but I love the ritual!’” According to the Song dynasty scholar Zhu Xi’s annotation: “sacrificial sheep” means live sheep. When the feudal lords reported to the ancestral temple and attended to government affairs on the first day of each month, it was called *gaoshuo*. Zi Gong (Duanmu Ci), seeing that the ruler of the state of Lu had already abolished the rite of *gaoshuo*, wished to do away with the sheep prepared for the rite as well; but Confucius thought that having the sheep could still preserve a little of the form of the ritual, hence this remark.

(1) Ci derives from the Music Bureau, and the Music Bureau derives from Poetry. The materials of the Greater and Lesser Elegantiae among the Four Sections of Poetry total one hundred and five. It is from refined material that the “Airs” derive their beauty and the “Hymns” their completion. From Poetry to the Music Bureau to ci, one must aspire to that single word “refinement” before one can stand tall and claim to be a true author. Therefore, when Zeng Duobo selected ci, he called them *Music Bureau Refined Lyrics*; Zhou Gongjin, being skilled at writing ci, inscribed his hall “Aim for Refinement.” The form of ci is circuitous and leisurely; unless woven through with refinement, it will almost inevitably drift away with the current and lose its proper bearing. (Preface to the *Collection of Refined Ci*)

The year before last, I went back for the Mid-Autumn Festival and heard that Sixth Aunt had come down with depression. Visiting Sixth Uncle’s home to look at paintings, I found it was indeed so. When others get depression, they do not like to talk; Sixth Aunt was talking nonstop, exhausting every unsatisfying event of her life; each and every unsatisfying event was related to Sixth Uncle. Sixth Uncle kept his head down and said nothing, only pointing at the paintings, looking at the paintings. Amid that torrent of words, how could I still have the mind to look at paintings? After casually glancing at two or three, I said I had guests at home at noon, and walked out of Sixth Uncle’s house.
