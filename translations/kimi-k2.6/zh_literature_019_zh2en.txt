Qin still visited the Gao family frequently. She came even more often now, almost every other day. Usually, however, she would return home as soon as night fell; if Mrs. Zhang also came to the Gao's to play cards, she could wait until the second watch to go back with her mother. Qin spent more time with Shuying, but they also had opportunities to play with Shuhua, Shuzhen, and the others. Sometimes these sisters would even arrange to have Yun come over to row boats in the garden and have picnics. When Yun arrived, she brought even more laughter to the group of young girls. Yun learned to row from Shuhua and borrowed translated novels from Shuying. She often laughed innocently, and her smile even brought a faint smile to Shuzhen's lips. Qin quietly guided and cared for them like an elder sister. She did her utmost to maintain an atmosphere of peace and joy among them. But when they spoke of Hui's affairs, even Qin would fall into gloomy meditation. At such times, Yun would hide her dimples, knit her brows tightly, and pout angrily. At such times, Shuying's clear eyes would again be clouded over by shadows. They could only express a little sympathy and indignation at Hui's fate, but could not lessen Hui's suffering in the slightest. Once they spoke of Hui, Qin and Shuying and her sisters would long to see her, especially when they learned that she had recently recovered from a new illness. They hoped she could chat and play with them as she had in the past. They wanted to invite Hui to the Gao household, but this was no easy matter. Hui had personally told Juexin that she could not decide for herself. Yet the hearts of young people are often unafraid of difficulties. They tried every means, and asked Mrs. Zhou and Juexin to help, and finally succeeded in inviting Hui. The day before, when Juexin had brought them this good news in advance, how excited and joyful these young girls had been.

The weather has turned warm lately, barely suitable for writing. Checking through Song dynasty notebooks, there are still over twenty kinds of ci criticism not yet copied out, and even more anecdotes on metal and stone inscriptions not yet copied; this year I should exert myself to complete them. The poems written before Liberation should also be polished this year and compiled into a definitive edition. Having reached the age of sixty, this matter cannot be delayed. (1963.2.23, p. 49)

Zhou Bapi struck a pose and lectured the farmhands. He said: "In my life, I, Zhou Bapi, have no particular hobbies; I just like watching others bend their backs working for me. As for what work to do and to what extent it counts as good enough, you all have eyes—use your own judgment. You might ask, when do we start work and when do we finish? That really puts me in a difficult spot. If I gave each of you an Omega watch, I might as well go work the fields myself. Listen up: you can stop work once it gets dark. By dark, I don't mean when the sun sets—I mean when you stand together and can't see each other's faces anymore, then you can knock off and go home. So when do you start work? You might think, since stopping is when it's dark, starting must be when it's light, right? Wrong. I'm not that stupid. In winter, dawn comes late; in summer, it comes early. And on cloudy, rainy days, it might still be as dark as the bottom of a pot at six or seven in the morning. Let me tell you plainly: don't bother about light or dark. As soon as you hear the village rooster crow, you can get up and go to the fields..." Having said his piece, Zhou Bapi slipped away and went back to his room to sleep.

In the early Qing, Huang Yuqi compiled the books of the entire Ming dynasty into the *Qianqingtang Bibliography*. In volume thirty-two, under the Ci and Qu category of the Collected Works section, there is listed the *Yuefu Huncheng Collection* in one hundred and five volumes, which should be what Zhou Mi recorded and what Wang Jide saw. One hundred and five volumes accords with Zhou Mi's words, "over a hundred huge volumes," so we know that the printed edition of this book still existed in complete form during the Ming dynasty. Both the Wang and Huang families referred to it as the *Huncheng Collection* (浑成), so the printed edition must have been thus; therefore, those who write it as *Huncheng* (混成) probably follow Zhou Mi's scribal error. The correct full title of the book should be the *Yuefu Huncheng Collection* (浑成), with the alternate title *Yuefu Daquan* (Great Collection of Yuefu); "huncheng" (浑成) means the same as "daquan" (great collection). This book is not seen in Qing dynasty bibliographies, nor has anyone ever quoted from it; it probably perished during the Ming-Qing transition.
