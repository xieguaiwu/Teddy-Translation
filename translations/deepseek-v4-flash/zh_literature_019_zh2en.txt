Qin still frequently visited the Gao family, now even more diligently—almost every other day. Usually, however, she would go home as soon as it got dark; unless Mrs. Zhang also came to play mahjong at the Gao house, in which case she could wait until the second watch and return with her mother. Qin spent most of her time with Shuying. But they also played with Shuhua, Shuzhen, and the others from time to time. Sometimes these sisters would plan together to invite Yun over for boating and dining in the garden. Whenever Yun arrived, she brought more laughter to the group of young women. Yun learned to row from Shuhua and borrowed translated novels from Shuying. She often laughed innocently, and her smile even brought a hint of a smile to Shuzhen’s lips. Qin, like an elder sister, quietly guided and cared for them. She did her utmost to maintain an atmosphere of peace and joy among them. But when they talked about Hui, even Qin would fall into gloomy silence. At such times, Yun would hide her dimples, furrow her brows tightly, and pout angrily. At such times, Shuying’s clear eyes would be clouded over. They could only express sympathy and indignation at Hui’s fate, without being able to alleviate her suffering in the slightest. Whenever they spoke of Hui, Qin and the sisters longed to see her, especially knowing she had recently recovered from an illness. They wished she could be with them as before, talking, laughing, and playing. Inviting Hui to the Gao family was no easy task. Hui herself had told Juexin that she was not her own mistress. But youthful hearts are often unafraid of difficulties. They tried every means, and with the help of Zhou and Juexin, they finally managed to invite Hui. The day before, when Juexin gave them this good news in advance, how excited and delighted these young women were!

Recently the weather has turned warmer, making it barely tolerable for writing. Checking my records, there are still over twenty types of Song dynasty notes and lyrics yet to be transcribed, and even more unrecorded inscriptions and anecdotes. I must complete them all this year. Poems written before the liberation should also be polished this year and compiled into a definitive edition. At sixty, this task cannot be delayed. (February 23, 1963, p. 49)

Zhou Balder pulled himself up to his full posture and started lecturing the farmhands. He said: "I, Zhou Balder, have no particular hobbies in life, except that I like to watch others bending over and working for me. As for what work to do and how well to do it, you all have eyes—just use your judgment. Now, you might ask when to start work and when to knock off. That's a tough question for me. If I gave each of you an Omega watch, I might as well go work the fields myself. Listen up: you can stop work once it gets dark. I don't mean when the sun sets. I mean when you're standing together but can't see each other's faces—then you can head home. As for starting work? You clever little minds might think: if knocking off means dark, then starting must mean dawn, right? Wrong. I, Zhou Balder, am not that stupid. In winter, dawn comes late; in summer, it comes early. And if it's overcast or rainy, it's still as dark as the bottom of a pot at six or seven in the morning. So, to put it plainly, don't worry about whether it's dawn or dark. When you hear the roosters crowing in the village, you get up and head to the fields..." Having said this, Zhou Balder's figure flickered, and he went back to his room to sleep.

In the early Qing dynasty, Huang Yuji compiled a catalog of Ming dynasty works titled the "Qianqingtang Bibliography." In its volume 32, under the category of poetry and ci lyrics, there is an entry for "Yuefu Huncheng Ji" in 105 fascicles, which corresponds to what Zhou Mi recorded and Wang Jide had seen. The 105 fascicles also match Zhou Mi's mention of "a large work of over a hundred fascicles," indicating that this book, in its printed edition, still existed in complete form during the Ming dynasty. Both Wang and Huang refer to it as "Huncheng Ji," so the printed edition must have been titled thus. The variant with "Hun" (mixed) instead of "Hun" (whole) is likely a scribal error by Zhou Mi. The proper full title of the book should be "Yuefu Huncheng Ji," with the alternate name "Yuefu Daquan," where "Huncheng" means "complete collection." This book is not recorded in Qing dynasty bibliographies, nor is it cited by anyone; it probably perished during the transition from Ming to Qing.
