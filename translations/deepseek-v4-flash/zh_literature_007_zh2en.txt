"Five Marquises"  
In the second year of the Heping era (27 BCE) under Emperor Cheng of Han (Liu Ao), the five maternal relatives Wang Tan, Wang Fengshi, Wang Gen, Wang Li, and Wang Shang were enfeoffed as marquises on the same day, collectively known as the "Five Marquises." According to the "Biographies of Wandering Knights" in the *Book of Han*, the Five Marquises kept many Confucian scholars and knights-errant, among whom the great knight Lou Hu (Lord Qing) was the most trusted, serving as the "guest of honor of the Five Marquises."

The aroma of millet porridge filled the little hut. The woman lifted the lid of the pot. To my amazement, there was a great deal of porridge in the pot—enough to fill three large bowls. She brought over three black bowls from the corner and began scooping with a wooden ladle whose edges were charred black. Scoop after scoop, scoop after scoop, scoop after scoop—she filled three large bowls, and there was still plenty left in the pot. I was puzzled, delighted, and utterly confused. Could all this porridge really have been made from just those few dozen grains of millet? What kind of person was this woman? A demon? A goddess? The two foxes that had dashed into the shrine during the downpour, drawn by the scent of the porridge, now walked boldly into our little hut. The vixen led the way, the fox followed, and between them wobbled three fluffy little cubs. They looked adorably naive and clumsy. "During thunder and lightning and torrential rain, beasts love to give birth"—that saying proved true indeed. The two adult foxes crouched before the pot, now lifting their heads to gaze at the woman with pleading eyes, now staring into the pot with greedy, hungry eyes. Their bellies rumbled—the sound of starvation. The three cubs nuzzled beneath the vixen, searching for her teats. The fox’s eyes were moist and vivid, as if he might speak at any moment. I knew what he would say if he could. The woman looked at the grand monk. He sighed and pushed his own large bowl of porridge toward the vixen. The woman followed suit, sliding her bowl over to the fox. The two foxes bowed their heads in thanks to the grand monk and the woman, then began to eat noisily. The porridge was hot, so they ate cautiously, their eyes glistening with tears. I felt awkward, looking at the porridge before me, uncertain whether I should eat it or not. The grand monk said, "Go ahead, eat." This was surely the finest porridge I had ever tasted, and I would never taste such good porridge again. The two foxes and I each ate three bowls of porridge. The foxes belched contentedly, then wobbled off with their cubs. By then, I noticed the pot was completely clean—not a single grain of rice left. I felt apologetic, but the grand monk had already sat down on the bed, counting his prayer beads as if asleep. The woman sat before the coal stove, fiddling with an iron poker. The faint firelight illuminated her face, making it vivid and expressive. She smiled softly, as if recalling fond memories, or perhaps lost in thought without remembering anything in particular. I patted my full belly and heard from the shrine outside the sound of the cubs nursing. I couldn’t hear the kittens nursing in the tree hollow, but I felt as though I could see them doing the same. I, too, felt a strong urge to nurse, but where were my teats? I had no desire to sleep. To resist the urge, I said, "Grand monk, I’ll continue my story."

I grew up in a remote region and had no idea what Manchu or Han meant; I’d only ever seen the phrase "Manchu-Han Banquet" on restaurant signs, and it never stirred any questions in me. I often heard tales of "this dynasty," but I’d never heard of the literary inquisition cases. The grand tours of the Qianlong Emperor to the south were rarely recounted; most often, people talked about "suppressing the Long Hairs." There was an elderly maidservant in my household who said she had been in her teens during the Long Hair rebellion. She told me the most stories about them, but she made no distinction between right and wrong, only saying the most fearsome things were three kinds: one was naturally the "Long Hairs," another was the "Short Hairs," and the third was the "Flowery Greens." Only later did I realize that the latter two were actually government troops, but in the experience of the ignorant masses, they were no different from the Long Hairs. It was a few educated men who pointed out to me the wickedness of the Long Hairs. My family had several county gazetteers, and when I happened to leaf through them, I found one or two fascicles listing the names of martyrs and chaste women who had died in the rebellion; a few members of my own clan had also been killed, and later were posthumously granted hereditary "Cloud Cavalry Commanders." Thus, I firmly concluded that the Long Hairs were wicked. Yet truly, "the heart is like surging waves": as time passed, based on my own experience and corroborated by the maidservant’s tales, I could no longer decide whether the murderers of those martyrs and chaste women were the Long Hairs or the "Short Hairs" and "Flowery Greens." I truly envied the happiness of the sage who "at forty had no doubts."
