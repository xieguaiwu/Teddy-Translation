The Stone Wall

The wall had stood for three hundred years, separating a sheep pasture from a dirt road. It was built without mortar, each stone fitted against the next by a farmer whose name had been forgotten. Over the centuries, the wall had sagged and bulged, but it had never fallen. Children had climbed it, lovers had sat on it, and sheep had rubbed against it until their wool caught on the rough edges. One day, the town council decided the wall was a hazard and voted to remove it. The bulldozer arrived on a Tuesday morning. An hour later, the wall was gone.

In the weeks following the wall's removal, the pasture changed. Sheep wandered onto the road. Wind carried topsoil away. The shape of the landscape shifted, subtly at first, then noticeably. The town council received complaints. Some people said they felt disoriented without the wall, as if a familiar landmark had been erased from their mental map. A movement began to rebuild it, using the original stones that had been piled in a nearby lot. Volunteers worked weekends, fitting the stones back together without mortar, as the original farmer had done three centuries before. By autumn, the wall stood again, not quite the same but close enough.
