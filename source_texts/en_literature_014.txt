The Translator

She translated poetry from a language that very few people spoke anymore. The poems were centuries old, written by a woman who had lived in isolation, composing verses for an audience that would not exist for another five hundred years. Her own life was quiet, almost monastic. She worked at a small desk by the window, a dictionary in one hand and a thesaurus in the other. The words were difficult, full of ambiguities and allusions that had lost their context. She sometimes wondered if she was translating the poems or writing her own.

She finished the translation after seven years. When she read it through, she realized that she had not so much translated the poems as rewritten them, filtering them through her own life, her own solitude, her own understanding of what it meant to be a woman speaking across centuries. She did not know if this was a betrayal of the original or the only possible form of fidelity. She sent the manuscript to a small press. They accepted it. When the book was published, only two hundred copies were sold. She did not mind in the slightest. She had done what the poems had asked of her.
