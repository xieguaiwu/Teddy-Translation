The Map Maker

He had been drawing maps since he was seven years old, starting with his bedroom, then his house, then the neighborhood. Now, at sixty-three, he was one of the country's foremost cartographers. But his passion was not the maps that were published. It was the maps no one would ever see — maps of places that existed only in memory. The street where he had bought ice cream as a child. The field where he had kissed a girl for the first time. The hospital room where his father had died. He drew them with extreme precision, as if accuracy could preserve what time was determined to erase.

His final map was of his wife's face. He drew it in pencil, very lightly, as if the pressure of his hand might erase the memory. The lines were soft and careful, the shadows gentle. He worked on it for months, erasing and redrawing, trying to capture the exact curve of her smile, the particular light in her eyes when she laughed. When he finished, he did not show it to anyone. He folded it carefully and placed it in a drawer beside the other maps, the ones of places that no longer existed except in his keeping.
