The Garden at Dusk

The garden was not large, but it held the evening light in a way that made it seem infinite. Old Mr. Morrison had planted every tree himself, forty years ago, when his hands were steady and his back did not ache. Now he sat on the bench near the rose bushes, watching the shadows lengthen across the grass. A blackbird sang from the elder tree, its song clear and unhurried. He thought about his wife, gone five years now, and how she had loved this hour of the day. The air smelled of damp earth and honeysuckle. Time moved differently here, he decided, slower and kinder.

He had built the garden for her, though she had died before the first rose bloomed. The neighbors said it was a waste, an old man tending flowers that no one would see. But he knew she saw them, somehow. Every evening, he sat on the bench and told her about the day, about the birds that visited the feeder, about the neighbor's cat that had learned to open the gate. The garden grew denser each year, the paths narrower, the flowers more abundant. It became a small wilderness in the heart of the suburb. Sometimes children came to the fence and stared at the riot of color inside. He did not mind. Let them look, he thought. Beauty does not need to be understood to be felt.
