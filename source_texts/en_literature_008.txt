The Last Night of Summer

They sat on the porch steps, two teenagers on the last night of summer, not speaking. The air was warm and heavy with the smell of cut grass. Fireflies blinked in the dark spaces between trees. Tomorrow, school would begin, and everything would change. But tonight, the summer was not quite over. One of them picked up a stone and threw it into the darkness, listening for the sound of it landing. They heard nothing. Some things, they were learning, disappear without a trace.

The next morning, they woke early and walked to the bus stop together. The bus came, and one of them got on. The other stood on the sidewalk, watching the bus disappear around the corner. The summer was over, as summers always are. But the memory of that last night, of the fireflies and the silence and the thrown stone that never landed, would stay with both of them longer than either would ever admit. They did not write to each other. They did not need to.

The quiet rhythms of daily life carried their own wisdom, though it was easy to miss amidst the noise of the world. Small gestures, repeated over time, accumulated into something meaningful. A garden tended daily, a letter preserved through decades, a craft passed from parent to child — these were the threads that held life together. In the end, what mattered was not the grand achievements or dramatic events, but the steady attention given to ordinary things. This was the truth that those who lived simply understood, and that the rest of the world, in its rush toward progress, had nearly forgotten.
