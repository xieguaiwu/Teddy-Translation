The Swimmer

Every morning, rain or shine, the old man swam in the cold waters of the lake. The doctors had told him to stop. His heart, they said, could not take the shock. But he went anyway, because the water was the only place where he felt truly alive. The cold erased everything, thought and worry and regret, leaving only the sensation of movement, of arms pulling through water, of breath coming in gasps. He swam to the buoy and back, a distance he had measured years ago. It was enough. It was always enough.

One morning, he did not come to the lake. The next morning, also not. People who had seen him every day for years noticed his absence, though none of them knew his name. The following week, a small notice appeared in the local newspaper: an obituary for a retired teacher who had loved to swim. His family had come from another city, had cleared out his small house, had left without fanfare. The lake continued to receive the morning light, indifferent to the loss of its most faithful visitor. But those who swam there felt something missing, a gap in the rhythm of their mornings. It was simply the only thing to do.
