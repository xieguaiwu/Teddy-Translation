The Beekeeper

She kept bees on the roof of her apartment building, thirty stories above the city streets. The bees flew down into the parks and gardens, collecting nectar from flowers that grew in the cracks of the urban landscape. The honey they produced tasted of everything and nothing, a flavor that could not be replicated. Her neighbors thought she was eccentric. The building management had tried to evict her. But the bees stayed, and so did she. In the summer, she gave jars of honey to anyone who asked. In the winter, she talked to the hives, telling them about the spring that would come.

The beekeeper died in her apartment, surrounded by the gentle hum of her hives. The building management, unsure what to do, called a beekeeper from the suburbs to remove the colonies. He found twenty-three hives, each one meticulously maintained. In a notebook, she had recorded years of observations: which flowers bloomed when, how the honey tasted each season, the personality of each queen. The suburban beekeeper kept the notebook and tried to follow her methods. But the honey he produced never tasted quite the same. Something of the city, of the roof, of her careful attention, could not be replicated.
