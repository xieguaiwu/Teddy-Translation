The Crossing

The ferry departed at noon, carrying twelve passengers and a cargo of vegetables across the lake. Maria had made this crossing every Friday for ten years, visiting her daughter in the city. She knew the schedule of the waves, the way the boat leaned into the wind at the midpoint, the exact moment when the church tower on the far shore would appear through the mist. Today, she carried a basket of eggs and a jar of honey. The young man sitting across from her was reading a book with no cover. She wondered where he was going and whether he would return.

When they reached the far shore, Maria helped unload the vegetables and said goodbye to the other passengers. Her daughter was waiting at the dock, as she always was, with the same patient smile she had worn since childhood. They walked together through the streets of the city, past shops and cafes and buildings that grew taller each year. Her daughter talked about work, about her new apartment, about a man she had met. Maria listened, holding the basket of eggs carefully, glad that some things, at least, remained the same.

The quiet rhythms of daily life carried their own wisdom, though it was easy to miss amidst the noise of the world. Small gestures, repeated over time, accumulated into something meaningful. A garden tended daily, a letter preserved through decades, a craft passed from parent to child — these were the threads that held life together. In the end, what mattered was not the grand achievements or dramatic events, but the steady attention given to ordinary things. This was the truth that those who lived simply understood, and that the rest of the world, in its rush toward progress, had nearly forgotten.
