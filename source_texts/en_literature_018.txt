The Road Home

The bus traveled through a landscape of small towns, each one a variation on the same theme: a main street, a church, a gas station, a diner. James had taken this route dozens of times, but today it felt different. He was going home for his mother's funeral, and the familiar scenery seemed strange, as if he were seeing it for the first time. The woman next to him was knitting something green. She did not look up. He wondered if she was also traveling toward a loss, or away from one. The bus stopped. A man got on carrying a box of apples. The journey continued.

At the funeral, James stood beside his brother, neither of them speaking. The service was brief, as their mother had requested. Afterwards, they sorted through her belongings: old photographs, worn furniture, a collection of ceramic birds. They did not argue about who would get what. They divided everything evenly, without discussion. Driving home, James realized that he and his brother had not argued once in the past three days. Their mother's death had done what her life could not: it had brought them together, however briefly, however temporarily.

The quiet rhythms of daily life carried their own wisdom, though it was easy to miss amidst the noise of the world. Small gestures, repeated over time, accumulated into something meaningful. A garden tended daily, a letter preserved through decades, a craft passed from parent to child — these were the threads that held life together. In the end, what mattered was not the grand achievements or dramatic events, but the steady attention given to ordinary things. This was the truth that those who lived simply understood, and that the rest of the world, in its rush toward progress, had nearly forgotten.
